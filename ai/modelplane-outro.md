So, stepping all the way back, what did we actually build here? We took a spread of Kubernetes clusters and treated them as one inference fleet. The platform side described the hardware. The developer described a model and its requirements. And the application reached it through one service, without following its replicas around the fleet.
For me, the most compelling idea in all of this is that fleet scheduler paired with the single front door. One model, its replicas placed across different clusters and regions, and yet reached as one simple service. That part is genuinely hard to pull off, and it's what sets a fleet-level control plane apart from serving a model on a single cluster.
And the job continues after the first response. We need to scale, roll out changes, handle unhealthy backends, and see what callers are experiencing. Modelplane provides building blocks for that: traffic weights and priorities, shared model-weight caches, and fleet metrics exported through OpenTelemetry when a telemetry destination is configured. Those are operational choices to configure and measure, not a reason to assume one working curl request means production is solved.
And look, it's early. The APIs are still evolving. The scheduler accounts conservatively at the node level, healthy placements are retained rather than constantly optimized, and no control plane can promise that a cloud has GPUs to rent. We saw that during preparation: AWS capacity was unavailable, and this GCP fleet was the working path. The platform has to expose those limits honestly.
The architecture is the lesson to take away. Keep providing GPU capacity separate from consuming it. Connect the two through a declarative, continuously reconciled contract. Keep the application's service identity independent of replica placement. Modelplane is one concrete way to do that on infrastructure you own.
